Endgame Tables

The last tricks of a deal are answered from precomputed tables: every 2-card and 3-card ending is one lookup, and 4-card endings are matched against a table of learned card patterns.

Where the tables sit in the search

ddfast asks yes/no questions: "does North-South take at least t of the remaining n tricks?" At the start of every trick it tries to settle the question without searching, in this order:

1 card

Play the trick out: four cards, one winner.

2 cards

One lookup in the 2-card table. Always answers.

3 cards

One lookup in the 3-card table. Always answers.

4+ cards

The transposition table of this deal, then DDS's quick-tricks and later-tricks bounds.

4 cards

The 4-card pattern table. A hit is copied into the deal's transposition table, where it is cheaper to find again.

otherwise

Search: try the leads.

A lookup returns the answer and the cards that decided it. The search above stores its own result as a pattern over just those cards, so a small deciding set makes that stored result apply to many more positions. The tables therefore do more than stop the search a few tricks early.

The three tables

All three are indexed the same way, so that equivalent endings share an entry:

  • From the leader's seat. One entry serves all four leaders.
  • Over the cards in play. The remaining cards are packed together (pext), so only their order matters, not their ranks. Two bit strings over the packed cards say who holds each one.
  • Trumps in a fixed place, side suits sorted by length. Endings that differ only in which side suit is which share one entry.

With two cards a hand there are 70 × 6 × 6 = 2,520 ways to deal eight given cards; with three, 924 × 20 × 20 = 369,600 ways to deal twelve, for each of 102 suit-length shapes and both strains. Each entry stores the answer and the smallest set of cards that decides it, for every target.

"At least one trick" depends only on who holds the ♦A, so that answer holds wherever the other eleven cards are. Three tricks depend on the ♠A, K and Q and the ♦A; the low cards never matter.

4 cards: learned patterns

A table of every 4-card ending would be far too large. The 4-card table holds patterns instead: each is a proved statement about every ending with given suit lengths in the four hands, naming only the owners of the cards that mattered and a bound on the leader's side's tricks. The patterns are learned when the table is built, by solving random 6-card endings for a fixed time and keeping every pattern proved for a 4-card ending on the way.

A lookup finds the patterns for the ending's suit lengths and tests eight at a time (AVX-512 on x86, Google Highway on Apple silicon): a pattern answers when its cards have the same owners in this ending and its bound settles the question. Because every pattern is proved, a hit is exact. When no pattern answers, the search goes on.

Patterns answer three of the four questions. The fourth has none (South can in fact take all four tricks), so the search plays that one out, and one trick later every ending is a single 3-card lookup.

What the tables are worth

With a table turned off, its endings are searched instead. On the 600-deal public set, one Zen 5 core (leave-one-out study):

Turned offNodesSlowdown
Both tables+56%1.30×
3-card table only+7.3%1.04×
4-card pattern table only+7.5%1.03×

The tables stand in for each other. Without one of them, the other, the transposition table and the bounds still catch most of the endings it would have answered. Only with both gone does the search grow by half. The tables matter more for small positions: ddfast is 13× DDS's speed on 7-card positions and 58× at 5 cards.

Building them

The first init() builds whatever is missing: the 3-card table in about 10 seconds on 8 cores, then the pattern table in up to 170 seconds on all cores, using up to about 5 GB of memory. The pattern table is filled for a fixed time, so tables built on different machines differ in size; the answers are exact either way.
